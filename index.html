Same Winds, Different Fires

How the Palisades and Eaton fires spread under one Santa Ana wind event, as seen by a weather satellite, January 7 to 9, 2025.

What happened

On January 7, 2025, strong, dry Santa Ana winds blew across Los Angeles. That morning, the Palisades fire started in the hills near the coast. That evening, the Eaton fire started in the foothills above Pasadena. Both fires burned under the same wind event, but they did not behave the same way.

Our question: if both fires had the same winds, how and why did they grow so differently?

What the satellite measures

  • Where fire is burning. NOAA's GOES-18 satellite photographs the western U.S. every 5 minutes. Each image is split into squares about 2 km (1.2 miles) wide, and the satellite flags squares that are on fire.
  • How strongly it is burning. For each burning square, it estimates fire power: the heat energy given off, in megawatts (MW). For scale, a large power plant produces about 1,000 MW.
  • Wind. It estimates wind by tracking how clouds and water vapor move between images.

How to use this page

  1. Press Play or drag the time slider to watch the fires grow.
  2. Switch the clock to "Line up both fires" to compare them from the moment each one started.
  3. Use the Show me buttons below to jump to the most important moments.
  4. Zoom and drag the map to look closely at one fire, and hover over any square, circle, star, arrow, or chart for details.

    About the data

    • Source: NOAA GOES-18 satellite, from the public NOAA archive on AWS. Fire data: the Fire Detection product (ABI-L2-FDCC). Wind data: Derived Motion Winds (ABI-L2-DMWC and ABI-L2-DMWVC).
    • Time window: Tuesday January 7, 8 AM to Thursday January 9, 8 AM, Pacific time. That is 576 satellite images, one every 5 minutes.
    • What we kept: only squares over the Los Angeles area that the satellite marked as fire. We dropped its lowest-confidence detections because they are often false alarms.
    • Which fire is which: we drew a box around each fire's area. Detections inside a box are labeled Palisades or Eaton, and everything else is shown in gray as "other fires."
    • Smoothing: the fire power chart shows a 30-minute rolling average, because single images jump around when smoke or clouds block the view.
    • Limits of the wind data:
    • Limits of the fire data: each square is about 2 km wide, so the map shows the general area burning, not exact fire edges. Thick smoke can hide fire for a few minutes, which causes some of the sudden drops in the chart.

    Write-up

    Motivation

    On January 7, 2025, the Palisades and Eaton fires started in Los Angeles on the same day, during the same Santa Ana wind event. Palisades started around 10:30 AM near the coast, and Eaton started around 6:18 PM in the foothills above Pasadena. When we looked at the satellite data for our checkpoint, the two fires did not act the same at all. Palisades peaked at about 25,500 MW, more than two and a half times Eaton's peak. But in the first twelve hours Eaton actually covered more ground. So our question became: if both fires had the same winds, why did they behave so differently? We used NOAA's GOES-18 satellite because it takes a picture every five minutes, which is often enough to watch both fires grow.

    Design rationale

    The map. At the checkpoint we planned a map with a time slider so you could watch the fires spread hour by hour, and it became the center of the page. Each faint square is one 2 km square the satellite flagged as burning at some point. We used squares because that's the actual size of what the satellite sees, and we didn't want the map to look more exact than the data is. The circles show squares that are burning right now, and bigger circles mean more fire power. We used a square-root scale so a circle's area matches its power, which keeps the few really hot squares from covering everything else. Stars mark where each fire started. A "How to read the map" key above the map explains what each color, shape, circle size, and wind symbol means.

    Color. We kept the same colors from our checkpoint graphs: orange for Palisades, blue for Eaton, and gray for smaller fires nearby like Hurst. The colors stay the same on the map, the charts, and the side cards, so you only learn them once. Orange and blue are also easy to tell apart for most people with color blindness. Wind is drawn in teal with standard weather-map wind barbs. The barb points toward where the wind is coming from, and more ticks mean faster wind. Barbs near the ground are drawn thicker than the ones high up. County outlines and city names like Malibu and Pasadena help you tell where you are on the map.

    The charts. Below the map is the fire energy line chart we planned at the checkpoint, plus a wind chart. Both use the same time axis as the slider, and a black line shows where you are. We used lines because two lines on the same axis make it easy to see which fire was bigger at any time.

    Interactions. The time slider and Play button let you watch both fires grow over the two days, since one static map can only show one moment. We added panning, zooming, and tooltips so people can look closely at each fire and get exact numbers. You can drag the map, zoom with the + and − buttons, or use the "Zoom to Palisades" and "Zoom to Eaton" buttons, because the fires are pretty small compared to the whole map. Hovering over a square, circle, star, wind barb, or chart shows details like the time, fire power, temperature, and how high up a wind reading was. We also added a "Line up both fires" clock. Eaton started almost 8 hours after Palisades, which makes them hard to compare in real time. Lining them up by hours since each one started lets you compare them at the same "age", which is how our third checkpoint graph worked. In that mode the wind compass splits into two dials, one for each fire's own time. The "What the data shows" box sums up our main findings, and each one has a Show me button that jumps the map to the moment it describes, so readers can check the claim themselves. The Jump to buttons do the same for key moments like each fire's start and peak. The side cards update with the slider and show how long each fire has been burning, its heat output right now, its highest heat output so far, and how many squares it has reached. You can also click or drag on the charts to move the map to that time. Last, there is an "Only winds near the ground" checkbox, which hides the high-altitude readings so you can see how few low-level readings the satellite actually got.

    What we changed from the checkpoint. We had six static graphs at the checkpoint, and we didn't bring all of them over. We dropped the spread-direction graph (Fig 4) because each fire only covered about 30 squares, which is too few to say for sure which way it spread. We also dropped the fire energy vs. wind speed scatter plot (Fig 6). In our video we said Eaton followed the wind more closely, but later we realized the wind readings came from different heights at different times (explained below), so that comparison wasn't fair. We also thought about drawing a smooth wind field across the whole map. But the satellite only gave us a few scattered wind readings each hour, so filling in the gaps would basically be making up data. Instead we only show the readings that actually exist.
