Same Winds, Different Fires

How the Palisades and Eaton fires spread under one Santa Ana wind event, as seen by a weather satellite, January 7 to 9, 2025.

What happened

On January 7, 2025, strong, dry Santa Ana winds blew across Los Angeles. That morning, the Palisades fire started in the hills near the coast. That evening, the Eaton fire started in the foothills above Pasadena. Both fires burned under the same wind event, but they did not behave the same way.

Our question: if both fires had the same winds, how and why did they grow so differently?

What the satellite measures

  • Where fire is burning. NOAA's GOES-18 satellite photographs the western U.S. every 5 minutes. Each image is split into squares about 2 km (1.2 miles) wide, and the satellite flags squares that are on fire.
  • How strongly it is burning. For each burning square, it estimates fire power: the heat energy given off, in megawatts (MW). For scale, a large power plant produces about 1,000 MW.
  • Wind. It estimates wind by tracking how clouds and water vapor move between images.

How to use this page

  1. Press Play or drag the time slider to watch the fires grow.
  2. Switch the clock to "Line up both fires" to compare them from the moment each one started.
  3. Use the Show me buttons below to jump to the most important moments.
  4. Zoom and drag the map to look closely at one fire, and hover over any square, circle, star, or chart for details.

    About the data

    • Source: NOAA GOES-18 satellite, from the public NOAA archive on AWS. Fire data: the Fire Detection product (ABI-L2-FDCC). Wind data: Derived Motion Winds (ABI-L2-DMWC and ABI-L2-DMWVC).
    • Time window: Tuesday January 7, 8 AM to Thursday January 9, 8 AM, Pacific time. That is 576 satellite images, one every 5 minutes.
    • What we kept: only squares over the Los Angeles area that the satellite marked as fire. We dropped its lowest-confidence detections because they are often false alarms.
    • Which fire is which: we drew a box around each fire's area. Detections inside a box are labeled Palisades or Eaton, and everything else is shown in gray as "other fires."
    • Smoothing: the fire power chart shows a 30-minute rolling average, because single images jump around when smoke or clouds block the view.
    • Limits of the wind data:
    • Limits of the fire data: each square is about 2 km wide, so the map shows the general area burning, not exact fire edges. Thick smoke can hide fire for a few minutes, which causes some of the sudden drops in the chart.

    Write-up

    Motivation

    The Palisades and Eaton fires began hours apart on January 7, 2025, during the same Santa Ana wind event. We focus on these two fires to ask: how did two nearby fires exposed to the same broad wind event grow differently? A time-based map makes it possible to compare where each fire was detected as it spread, rather than looking only at its final footprint.

    Design rationale

    Faded squares mark locations where fire was detected earlier; circles mark detections in the current five-minute scan. This separates the accumulated footprint from current activity. Circle area increases with estimated fire radiative power, so larger circles indicate more heat output. Palisades and Eaton have distinct colors, while nearby detections outside their mapped areas are gray. Stars mark reported ignition locations.

    The time slider and Play control show how the fires change over time. Real time preserves when each fire actually occurred; Line up both fires resets the clock to each fire's ignition so their growth can be compared at the same age. The Show me buttons provide direct access to selected moments, while pan, zoom, and hover support closer inspection. The wind compass summarizes regional direction and speed. Map streamlines are interpolated from satellite readings and fade where samples are sparse; their gentle meander is a visual aid, not a measured gust pattern.

    A static final-footprint map would hide the timing and sequence of growth, so we used an interactive timeline instead. We also included both real-time and ignition-aligned views: the first preserves event chronology, while the second makes growth-stage comparisons easier. Because wind observations are sparse, we avoid presenting the streamlines as a fully observed continuous field.

    Data transformations

    We filtered fire detections to the Los Angeles study area and removed the lowest-confidence detections. Locations inside the Palisades and Eaton study boxes are assigned to those fires; remaining detections are grouped as other fires. Repeated detections at a location form its burned-area square, positioned by its first detection. Current-fire circles use detections from the active five-minute scan and are sized by fire radiative power. The fire-power time series uses a 30-minute rolling average to reduce scan-to-scan variation.

    Wind readings are grouped by hour for the map and regional summaries. Readings at 700 hPa or higher are treated as nearer the ground; the rest are higher-altitude observations. The map estimates local flow by interpolating nearby wind vectors, separately by altitude group, and draws streamlines only where observations provide support. Their small meander is a styling choice; the source data does not measure continuous paths. Dry air limits the satellite's ability to track wind, and relatively few readings are near the ground, so the wind display describes broad patterns rather than exact conditions at either fire.
