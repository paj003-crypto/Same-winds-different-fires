Same Winds, Different Fires

How the Palisades and Eaton fires spread under one Santa Ana wind event, as seen by a weather satellite, January 7 to 9, 2025.

What happened

On January 7, 2025, strong, dry Santa Ana winds blew across Los Angeles. That morning, the Palisades fire started in the hills near the coast. That evening, the Eaton fire started in the foothills above Pasadena. Both fires burned under the same wind event, but they did not behave the same way.

Our question: if both fires had the same winds, how and why did they grow so differently?

What the satellite measures

  • Where fire is burning. NOAA's GOES-18 satellite photographs the western U.S. every 5 minutes. Each image is split into squares about 2 km (1.2 miles) wide, and the satellite flags squares that are on fire.
  • How strongly it is burning. For each burning square, it estimates fire power: the heat energy given off, in megawatts (MW). For scale, a large power plant produces about 1,000 MW.
  • Wind. It estimates wind by tracking how clouds and water vapor move between images.

How to use this page

  1. Press Play or drag the time slider to watch the fires grow.
  2. Switch the clock to "Line up both fires" to compare them from the moment each one started.
  3. Use the Show me buttons below to jump to the most important moments.
  4. Zoom and drag the map to look closely at one fire, and hover over any square, circle, star, or chart for details.

    About the data

    • Source: NOAA GOES-18 satellite, from the public NOAA archive on AWS. Fire data: the Fire Detection product (ABI-L2-FDCC). Wind data: Derived Motion Winds (ABI-L2-DMWC and ABI-L2-DMWVC).
    • Time window: Tuesday January 7, 8 AM to Thursday January 9, 8 AM, Pacific time. That is 576 satellite images, one every 5 minutes.
    • What we kept: only squares over the Los Angeles area that the satellite marked as fire. We dropped its lowest-confidence detections because they are often false alarms.
    • Which fire is which: we drew a box around each fire's area. Detections inside a box are labeled Palisades or Eaton, and everything else is shown in gray as "other fires."
    • Smoothing: the fire power chart shows a 30-minute rolling average, because single images jump around when smoke or clouds block the view.
    • Limits of the wind data:
    • Limits of the fire data: each square is about 2 km wide, so the map shows the general area burning, not exact fire edges. Thick smoke can hide fire for a few minutes, which causes some of the sudden drops in the chart.

    Write-up

    Motivation

    Although the Palisades and Eaton fires started on January 7, 2025 eight hours apart during the same event, they certainly didn’t grow the same way. The question we came up with was: how did the two nearby fires grow so differently despite coming from the same winds? We created a map that changes over time, since a burn map doesn’t show clearly how and when each fire spread.

    Design rationale

    Visual encodings

    In our visualization, color represents the fire (shows which fire). The Palisades fire is represented with orange and the Eaton is represented with blue, since these two colors are easily distinguishable, even for colorblind people. Since there were still other nearby fires, we used gray to represent them, since they weren’t the main focus of the visualization. The time is shown through shape. Faded squares show earlier detection, and solid circles are burning in the current five-minutes (current scan). Our first visualiation version showed one dot per detection, and the dots stacked, so we show each spot only once (as a square). The circle area shows the heat output of the fire, since color was already used for distinguishing the fires. Finally, the black stars show where each of the fires started.

    Interactions

    In the visualization, the slider and the Play button allow users to watch the fires as they grow. “Real time” in the visualization shows that both fires burned at the same time, and “Line up the fires” compares them at the same age (fire age). The “Show me” buttons jump to key moments in the life cycle. After receiving feedback in the checkpoint, we added more actions, like zooming, panning, as well as more detailed tooltips since each 2km square was small compared to all of Los Angeles. The wind compass shows the average wind per hour, and the lines on the map are estimated from readings, so the curves mainly serve aesthetic purposes.

    Other options considered

    A static map wouldn’t have worked very well because it wouldn’t show how the fires grew as time passed. Also, only one clock would either make the fires harder to compare by age, or hide that they were burning at the same time. We also considered a full, more smooth wind pattern since it would look better than our readings, but the implementation would have been much more complicated.

    Data transformations

    For our map, we focused only on fire detections in the Los Angeles area. We removed the ones that the satellite was the least sure about since those were the ones that were most likely to be false alarms. We used a box around each fire to sort the fire detections into Palisades, Eaton, and other fires. Each spot that burned shows up one time as a square on the map. For the fire power chart, we used a 30-minute average because the smoke and clouds could easily change the data. We also grouped the wind data by hour and split it into winds close to the ground (700 hPa or higher) and winds higher up above the ground. Since the air was very dry, the satellite found only a few wind readings. Because of this, we mainly showed the general wind direction instead of the exact wind conditions at each fire.

    Development process

    We worked on every part of the project together. This included cleaning the data and making our exploratory graphs in Google Colab and then building the D3 page and writing the write-up. When we first started we were looking at files from the wrong satellite and had to fix that before we could move forward. We also had to convert the satellite's scan angles into latitude and longitude so that each fire would appear in the correct place on the map. Another challenge was the limited wind data and it made it difficult to show detailed wind patterns. After we received feedback on our checkpoint we made several rounds of changes and most of them were to how the wind is shown on the map. In total we spent about 12 hours working on the project together and that is about 48 people-hours across our team of four. Preparing the data took the most time.
